Chapter 27: Project Scheduling

Summary of Pressman & Maxim, Software Engineering: A Practitioner's Approach, 7th ed.

What it is
Turning a plan into a network of software tasks, assigning who owns each one, and adjusting the network as risks become real. In short: connecting the dots between estimation and delivery.
Who does it
Project managers, drawing on information from the engineers doing the work — and engineers themselves at the individual task level.
Why it matters
Tasks run in parallel and depend on each other in ways that are nearly impossible to track without a schedule, especially on anything larger than a toy project.
Work product
A project schedule (task network + time-line chart) plus the tracking data that goes with it.

27.1Why software ships late

Pressman opens with a familiar story: an engineer reports "90% done" for the entire back half of a project. The chapter traces late delivery to a short list of root causes — deadlines set by people outside the team, requirements that change without a matching schedule change, honest underestimates of effort, risks nobody planned for, technical or human surprises, miscommunication, and management being slow to admit a project is slipping.

When a deadline genuinely can't be met, the recommended move isn't to accept it or to refuse it — it's to estimate honestly from historical data, propose an incremental delivery that hits the deadline with reduced scope, and bring that trade-off to the customer rather than quietly hoping the numbers work out.

27.2Basic scheduling principles

Every schedule rests on the same seven ideas:

  • Compartmentalization — break the project into manageable activities and tasks.
  • Interdependency — work out which tasks must be sequential and which can run in parallel.
  • Time allocation — give every task a start date, an end date, and an effort budget.
  • Effort validation — never schedule more concurrent effort than the team actually has capacity for.
  • Defined responsibilities — every task belongs to a named person.
  • Defined outcomes — every task produces a concrete work product.
  • Defined milestones — outcomes get reviewed and approved, and that review is the milestone.

People, effort, and the PNR curve

Adding people to a late project usually makes it later — new hires need ramp-up time from the very people who are already behind, and every additional person adds communication overhead. The Putnam-Norden-Rayleigh curve formalizes this: effort rises sharply as you try to compress delivery time, and pushing much past about 75% of the "natural" schedule moves the project into what Pressman calls the impossible region. The flip side is more useful than it sounds — stretching a deadline can cut required effort dramatically. One worked example in the chapter: a 12 person-year project needing 8 people to finish in 1.3 years could instead finish in 1.75 years with only 4 people.

Where the effort actually goes

The traditional rule of thumb is 40–20–40: 40% of effort on front-end analysis and design, 20% on coding, 40% on back-end testing. In practice the ranges are wider — planning is usually just 2–3%, requirements 10–25%, design 20–25%, coding 15–20%, and testing 30–40% (higher still for safety-critical software). The headline takeaway: coding is the smallest line item, not the biggest.

27.3–27.4Task sets and task networks

A task set is the collection of tasks, milestones, work products, and quality checks needed to finish a given kind of project — concept development, new application, enhancement, maintenance, or reengineering each call for a different level of rigor. A macroscopic schedule (major actions only) gets refined into a detailed schedule as the project proceeds, by breaking each action into subtasks with their own reviews and outcomes.

A task network (or activity network) is the graph that shows how those tasks depend on each other — which is exactly the input a scheduling tool needs to compute the critical path: the chain of tasks that determines how long the whole project takes.

27.5Scheduling, time-lines, and tracking

PERT and the critical path method (CPM) are the two classic techniques here. Both take the task network as input and let you find the critical path, generate "most likely" time estimates, and calculate a time window for each task. The visual output most teams actually use is a time-line (Gantt) chart: tasks down the left side, bars across the calendar, diamonds marking milestones, overlapping bars showing where work runs concurrently.

Tracking progress against that chart happens through status meetings, review outcomes, comparing planned vs. actual start dates, informal check-ins, and — for a quantitative view — earned value analysis (below). When a deadline is truly fixed and won't move, teams sometimes use time-boxing: work on a task stops at its boundary (±10%) regardless of whether it's "done," and anything unfinished is deferred to the next increment.

Object-oriented & WebApp projects

OO projects are iterative, so instead of dated milestones the chapter defines completion criteria: analysis is "done" when classes, attributes, and relationships are reviewed; design is "done" when subsystems and responsibilities are assigned and reviewed; and so on through programming and testing.

WebApp scheduling works at two levels too: a macroscopic schedule maps out when each increment (e.g., product info, then ordering, then account access) ships, and each increment is then broken into its own detailed task list — design the interface, design the navigation, review with stakeholders, and so on — once that increment is actually underway.

27.6Earned value analysis

Earned value analysis (EVA) turns "percent complete" into a number instead of a gut feeling, and Pressman notes it's considered reliable from as little as 15% into a project.

27.7Takeaway

Scheduling is where estimation and risk analysis turn into an actual road map. Compartmentalize the process, build the task network, find the critical path, generate a time-line chart, and then track against it — one day at a time, since Fred Brooks's answer for how projects fall behind is exactly that simple.
